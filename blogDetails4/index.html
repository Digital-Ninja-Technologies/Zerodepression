How to help a friend who is depressed

7 October 2026 · ZeroDepression team · 5 min read

Maybe your friend has stopped replying to messages. Maybe they cancel plans at the last minute, or they still show up but the light has gone out of their eyes. You can tell something is wrong, and you want to help, but you are scared of saying the wrong thing. That fear is normal, and the good news is that you don't need to be a counsellor to make a real difference. Most of what helps is simple: noticing, showing up, and listening.

Notice the signs

Depression doesn't always look like crying. In a friend it can look like:

  • Pulling away from people, group chats and things they used to enjoy
  • Sleeping much more or much less than usual
  • Seeming tired, flat or easily irritated most of the time
  • Talking about themselves as a failure or a burden
  • Struggling to keep up with work, school or daily tasks

One bad week is not depression. But if these changes last two weeks or more, it's worth checking in.

Start the conversation

Pick a quiet moment when you are alone together, or send a private message. Keep it simple and about what you have noticed, not about what you think is wrong with them. For example: "You've seemed really down lately and I've been thinking about you. How are you actually doing?"

If they say "I'm fine", you don't have to push. Let them know the door is open: "Okay. If that changes, I'm here, no judgement." Many people need to be asked more than once before they feel safe to open up.

Listen more than you talk

When your friend starts to share, your job is not to fix it. It is to make them feel heard. Put your phone away, let silences happen, and reflect back what you hear: "That sounds exhausting." "I'm glad you told me."

Try to avoid phrases that, however well meant, make people feel dismissed:

  • "Just pray about it" or "Just be positive"
  • "Other people have it worse"
  • "Na sapa, no be depression"
  • "Snap out of it"

Faith, hope and perspective can all be part of healing, but depression is not a choice or a lack of effort. Being told to cheer up usually makes a person feel more alone.

Offer practical help

When someone is depressed, small tasks can feel enormous. Instead of the general "Let me know if you need anything", offer something specific: bring food, go for a short walk together, help them reply to an email they've been avoiding, or sit with them while they make a call. Specific offers are much easier to say yes to.

Encourage support, gently

You can be a big part of your friend's support, but you shouldn't be all of it. Encourage them to talk to someone trained to help. You could say: "Talking to a counsellor might take some of the weight off. Do you want me to sit with you while you call?" ZeroDepression's counsellors are free and anonymous on 0800 1100 2200, and your friend can also chat with us anonymously if talking out loud feels too hard.

Take talk of suicide seriously

If your friend talks about wanting to die, being better off gone, or hurting themselves, don't brush it aside or promise to keep it secret. Asking directly, "Are you thinking about ending your life?", does not put the idea in their head. It shows you are taking them seriously. If they are in immediate danger, stay with them and call 112 or take them to the nearest hospital.

Look after yourself too

Supporting someone through depression can be draining. It's okay to have limits, to rest, and to talk to someone about how it is affecting you. You can call us too. You will be a better support to your friend if you are not running on empty.

You don't have to have the perfect words. Checking in, listening without judging, and staying close are often what someone remembers most when they look back on their darkest days.
